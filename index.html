1.  Off-policy = forgetting?

A long-standing goal of AI is a model that continually learns and improves itself. Today's models are heavily post-trained with RL, and the simplest way to teach them something new is supervised finetuning (SFT) on the new data. But SFT on such models often forgets and fails to generalize, so the conventional wisdom is that continual learning needs on-policy training, and many works try to make the data more on-policy, e.g., on-policy self-distillation (OPSD), Pedagogical RL, and SFT with sampling.

In practice, it is hard to balance on-policyness and data quality, while the data that carry new knowledge or capabilities are usually off-policy: new facts after the pretraining cutoff, expert demonstrations, and synthetic data for self-improvement. What if on-policy training is not necessary?

2.  Model merging alone mitigates forgetting

Our premise is that simply merging the SFT model back toward the original post-trained model, θmerge = (1 − λ) θpost + λ θ′post, makes SFT a strong baseline. Without merging, neither SFT nor OPSD beats the post-trained model. With merging, SFT generalizes much better, and OPSD loses its edge over SFT despite its expensive on-policy sampling.

3.  Grafting

Merging only scales the update down without changing its direction, so SFT was learning something useful. It hurts because it interferes with existing capabilities.

Where does the interference come from? Training on narrow distributions (e.g., post-training, or even late pretraining) skews which task a model infers its inputs to come from (Kotha et al., 2024). So finetuning later checkpoints routes more of the new learning signal to the parameters that support existing capabilities. This suggests learning the update on an earlier checkpoint instead. But how?

Task arithmetic (Ilharco et al., 2023) shows that an update need not be derived from the model it is applied to. Based on this, we change where the update is learned and how it is applied. We call the method grafting:

  1. learn the update on an earlier donor model θdonor;
  2. scale the weight update by λ and apply it to the post-trained model;
  3. optionally, mask the most sensitive weight directions (estimated with the Fisher information on old-task data) when the new data distribution is far from the post-trained model.

θ(λ, ρ) = θpost + λ · Mρ ⊙ (θdonor-ft − θdonor)

Here θdonor-ft is the donor after finetuning on the new data, and the mask Mρ zeroes the top-ρ fraction of Fisher-weighted directions of the update. Masking as few as ρ ≤ 1% of directions is enough.

Which donor model?

We want a donor that is early enough to see less interference, but late enough to stay close to the final model. OLMo-3 releases many intermediate checkpoints, which lets us study this. Each post-training stage (SFT, DPO, RL) makes the model harder to finetune, and the best donor sits before the end of pretraining.

4.  Results

We study settings where off-policy data arises naturally. For math, the question is where the thinking traces come from, and we study three sources:

  • Distillation from expert traces. On hard problems the model cannot solve, the only way to learn is from a stronger model, so we use traces written by DeepSeek-V4-Pro.
  • Self-improvement with STaR. Without a stronger model, the model can still learn from its own successes: we rejection-sample its attempts and keep one verified trace per solved problem. This requires that the model can solve the problem with a large budget.
  • Self-improvement with Pedagogical RL. Solutions from humans or expert systems rarely come with thinking traces. Pedagogical RL trains a copy of the model that sees the reference solution to write thinking traces the model can learn from, so the model self-improves from solutions alone.

For knowledge injection, the new data are facts absent from prior training, as for any fact after the pretraining cutoff. We use invented entities so that no fact can leak from pretraining, and the question-answer form is far from how a reasoning model writes.

In all settings, we assume access only to the data, not to the weights or logits of a stronger teacher, so on-policy distillation from a teacher is not possible. New-task performance is measured on held-out data from the new task; old-task performance is the mean over AIME 2024, AIME 2025 and HMMT 2025.

5.  Takeaways

We asked whether continual learning for RL-trained models has to be on-policy, and our results suggest that it does not.

  • SFT learns a useful signal but applies it badly. Merging the SFT model back toward the post-trained model improves both old and new task performance, so the update is useful; the damage comes from interference with existing capabilities.
  • Where the update is learned matters. Grafting learns the update on an earlier donor checkpoint, scales it, and optionally masks the most sensitive directions. In every setting we study, with two model families and three sizes, it dominates both SFT and OPSD on new and old tasks, without on-policy sampling.
  • The donor is a knob. Each post-training stage makes the model a worse donor, and the best donors are checkpoints before the end of pretraining.

Open questions include how to choose the donor checkpoint in a principled way, and how to predict λ and ρ instead of sweeping them. More broadly, since continual learning applications often involve off-policy experiences, we hope our work inspires more effort on making off-policy training generalize better.
